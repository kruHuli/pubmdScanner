Why does handwriting feel different?
Handwriting vs. typing sounds like an old debate. But over the last few months, I have been reaching for pen and paper again. Not because it’s romantic but because it’s given me some real insight.

I noticed something : I think differently when I write by hand.

The ideas come out flooding, but writing them down feels like I’ve understood something differently. So I started wondering: is handwriting just a slower way to get words onto a page or is there more to it? What does the research say?

Your brain does not treat them the same way
Researchers have looked at this using EEG, a method that measures electrical activity in the brain. When they compared handwriting and typing, they found that handwriting produced richer brain connectivity in networks associated with learning and memory.

That does not mean typing is useless. It means typing and handwriting are not the same cognitive act. They both produce words, but they do not ask the same thing of the brain.

Typing removes friction
Typing, for all its glory, is efficient. I say this while chipping away at my keyboard. Every letter is produced in almost the same way. You can barely feel the difference between typing a W and typing an S. Your fingers move quickly. The words appear on the screen like magic.

That is the advantage of typing. It removes friction. And for many tasks, that is exactly what you want. If you are drafting a long essay, replying to emails, editing a document, coding, or capturing information quickly, the keyboard is a brilliant tool.

But learning is not only about capturing information. Learning requires the brain to work with information.

Handwriting makes the brain steer
Handwriting is different. Your brain has to control the pressure of the pen. It has to respond to the paper, the ink, the angle of your hand. It has to guide every curve and stroke. It has to adjust spacing, shape, and direction as you write.

No two letters ever look exactly the same. An S at the beginning of a word does not flow exactly like an 𝓈 in the middle of a word. The shape changes slightly depending on what came before it and what comes after it.

Your brain is constantly calculating, calibrating, and adjusting. Typing is like driving on a straight highway. Handwriting is taking the long winding road. Cruise control vs. actively steering around every turn.

Why speed matters
There is a second part to this story. Handwriting is not simply a more sensory experience. It is slower. And the brain has to adapt to a slower output.

A landmark note-taking study found that students who took notes by hand performed better on conceptual questions than students who took notes on laptops. The laptop note-takers wrote more. They captured more information. But they also tended to copy the lecture almost word for word.

The students writing by hand could not keep up in the same way. So they had to filter. They had to decide what mattered. They had to summarize, paraphrase, and organize the information as they wrote.

In other words, handwriting made note-taking less like transcription and more like thinking.

More notes does not mean better notes
The laptop note-takers captured more. But capturing more did not necessarily mean understanding more.

We often confuse information capture with learning. They are not the same thing. Typing can make you feel like you are doing more because the page fills quickly. You feel like you have done a good job. It feels satisfying, even.

But if the brain is mostly transcribing, it may not be processing the material deeply. Handwriting introduces a useful constraint. You cannot write everything down. You have to choose the key ideas and concepts.

So which one wins?
If the goal is speed, typing wins. If the goal is exact transcription, typing wins. If the goal is editing, searching, organizing, or producing a lot of text, typing wins.

But if the goal is learning, memory, or working through an idea, handwriting has the edge. Not because it is old-fashioned. Because it makes the brain do more of the work.

So when I need to capture something quickly, I still type. But when I need to understand something, remember something, or work through an idea, I am starting with the pen.

Not because paper is better. Because for certain kinds of thinking, the pen asks more of the brain.

The Battle Between Your Impulses and Your Focus
Your attention is caught in a constant battle. On one side: automatic impulses that happen without effort. On the other: intentional focus that requires conscious control. These two systems are always competing for dominance.

Scientists have a name for this collision — the Stroop Effect — and a way to measure it using Stroop Test. Understanding how they measure it reveals something fundamental about how your attention actually works.

The Question Stroop Asked



Stroop Test Card on a Desk
In 1935, American psychologist John Ridley Stroop published his landmark study, ‘Studies of Interference in Serial Verbal Reactions,’ in the Journal of Experimental Psychology. He wasn’t trying to measure attention broadly. He was asking a specific question: How much does an overlearned automatic task interfere with an intentional one?

Reading is automatic. After you master it around age six or seven, your brain processes written words almost instantly without conscious effort. It’s fast, efficient, and nearly impossible to suppress.

Color naming, by contrast, is deliberate. It requires attention and intention.

Stroop wanted to quantify the cost: If someone has to name a color while the automatic act of reading is fighting them, how much slower do they get?

The Experiment
The setup is a simple three-step test.

Step 1: Color names are printed in black ink. Read the words aloud.

Step 2: Color patches are shown. Name the colors aloud.

Step 3: Color names are printed in conflicting ink colors. Name the ink color, not the word.




In Stroop’s original data, naming 100 colors took 63.3 seconds in the control condition and 110.3 seconds in the incongruent condition — a 47-second increase, or about 74% slower. That delay is the measurable signature of the Stroop Effect. The interference is not vague or metaphorical. It is measurable, quantifiable, and repeatable.

Over 99% of incongruent trials took longer than the corresponding control trials.

Stroop’s original explanation: adults practice reading far more than naming colors, so the reading response is simply stronger and more automatic. When two responses compete, the stronger one wins — and suppressing it costs time.

Why the Effect Varies Between People
High vs Low Interference Individuals:

Not everyone experiences the same amount of interference. Some people are more easily pulled toward the irrelevant word, while others are better at narrowing attention to the ink color and suppressing distraction.

The effect varies between people because High Interference (HI) individuals exhibit broadened attention—they absorb incidental details but struggle to inhibit distractions—whereas Low Interference (LI) individuals can narrow focus, suppressing irrelevant stimuli more effectively.

The Variants: Interference Takes Many Forms
The Stroop Effect became a template for studying all kinds of mental interference:

Directional Stroop: Identify the direction an arrow points while ignoring a conflicting word like “LEFT” embedded inside it.

Counting Stroop: Count the number of digits on a screen while ignoring what the digits actually are.

Emotional Stroop: Use emotionally loaded words like “death” or “failure.” These tasks are often used to study attentional bias — the way emotionally significant information can capture mental resources.

Each variant isolates a different dimension of attention and interference.

Real-World Implications
Some research suggests bilingual people consistently outperform monolinguals on Stroop tasks. Because their brains are trained daily to switch between languages and inhibit the one not in use, they develop stronger conflict detection and greater mental endurance. Although the evidence is mixed, this is a well-demonstrated example of real-life Stroop Effect.

The Stroop test also has clinical uses, but it isn’t a stand-alone diagnostic tool. Clinically, Stroop-style tasks are used as one measure of inhibitory control, processing speed, and executive function.

The Takeaway
Stroop’s simple task revealed something fundamental: the battle between automatic and intentional is real, measurable, and constant. Your attention is a finite resource locked in negotiation between what your brain does automatically and what you’re trying to do intentionally.

Once you understand this mechanism, you start seeing it everywhere — in the pull of your phone, in the paragraph you’ve read three times, in the fatigue at the end of a day full of competing demands.

You’ve probably seen a box breathing tutorial. Or the 4-7-8 method. Maybe both, with competing claims about which one is “better.”

Here’s what nobody mentions: both protocols were tested under the constraints of how research actually works. You take a small group of people, apply one fixed protocol, compare it against a control, and if everything holds up under scrutiny, you publish the outcome. That structure gives a study its validity. But it also limits it.

A 2023 Stanford study (Balban et al.) compared box breathing against cyclic sighing and mindfulness meditation. Box breathing reduced stress — but cyclic sighing outperformed it on both mood and physiological measures. The study couldn’t test whether starting without any hold might have worked better for some participants, or whether a 4-2-4-2 pattern would’ve shown different results. Researchers don’t get to run dozens of permutations on the same group and still call it a clean study.

You, on the other hand, are not bound by the limits of research!

Why this matters for your brain

Slow, controlled breathing activates the prefrontal cortex — the region behind focus, emotional regulation, and decision-making — while simultaneously increasing heart rate variability, a marker of parasympathetic nervous system activity (Zaccaro et al., 2018). Most interventions target one or the other. Breathing reaches both.

CO2 tolerance — your body's ability to stay calm as carbon dioxide rises — is trainable. Research shows that even six weeks of restricted breathing practice in healthy adults produced a 45% reduction in ventilatory response to CO2, meaning their bodies stopped overreacting to normal fluctuations (Lemaître et al., 2013). When your CO2 tolerance is low, you tend to over-breathe — fast, shallow, chest-dominant. That pattern keeps the sympathetic nervous system constantly triggered. Training tolerance through progressive breath holds helps normalize breath rate and supports the shift toward parasympathetic balance.

Form before heavy weights

Asking someone to hold their breath for four or seven counts before they understand how their breath actually moves is like handing someone a loaded barbell before they’ve learned how to lift.

That’s why I teach the mechanics first. How the diaphragm descends on your inhale and rebounds on your exhale. How a full breath isn’t just your belly pushing forward — it’s your ribs expanding laterally and into your back, a 360-degree expansion. (I’ve written about this in detail here and here.)

Most people are chest-breathing and bracing without realizing it. Until that changes, adding a breath hold on top just reinforces the tension.

Why I rarely start with breath holds

We already hold our breath constantly. In 2008, tech researcher Linda Stone coined the term “email apnea” after observing that roughly 80% of people unconsciously hold their breath or shift to shallow breathing while reading email. Breath holding is already woven into most people’s days with screen apnea, stress bracing, shallow breathing through a difficult conversation

That habitual, unconscious breath holding doesn’t come with the relaxed vasodilatory response that makes controlled retention useful. When you hold your breath while tense, your nervous system is likely in sympathetic response and you may not receive the cerebrovascular benefits that are presented with slow breathing.

The experiment

What we need is a progressive approach. Same principle as physical training — you find where you are and build from there. What’s being loaded isn’t a muscle. It’s your nervous system’s ability to tolerate a pause in airflow without bracing against it.

Try this over a week or two. The goal isn’t to reach Stage 4 as fast as possible. The goal is to notice what happens at each stage.





Stage 1 (4-0-4-0): No hold. Just breathe. Move on when you can do 8–10 rounds with no tension in your jaw, shoulders, or belly.

Stage 2 (4-1-4-1): A single count of pause — not a clamp, not a brace. Move on when the pause doesn’t make your next inhale feel urgent.

Stage 3 (4-2-4-2): Two counts. Move on when you stay relaxed through the full cycle.

Stage 4 (4-4-4-4): Full box breathing.

If your jaw clenches, your shoulders lift, or your exhale gets forced, take a step back and make some notes.

When this doesn’t apply

If you’re working with a therapist and they’ve prescribed a specific breathing protocol, that structure is doing something intentional. Clinical settings often need the rigidity that self-experimentation doesn’t.

Research gives us frameworks. Good ones. But a protocol tested on a group of strangers under controlled conditions is a starting point. Training your brain, like training your body, isn’t about finding the perfect protocol. It’s about finding where you are and building from there.

Nobody’s going to publish a study with a sample size of one. That doesn’t make the findings any less valid.
